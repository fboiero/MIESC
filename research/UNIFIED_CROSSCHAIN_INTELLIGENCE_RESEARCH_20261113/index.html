    Does Security Intelligence Transfer Across Execution Models? — A 2027 Research Program¶

    Fernando Boiero — MIESC · Multi-layer Intelligent Evaluation for Smart Contracts · github.com/fboiero/MIESC Version 2026-11-14 · Applies to MIESC ≥ 6.8.0 · Status: research program (forward-looking)

    Abstract¶

    Smart-contract security tooling is sharply asymmetric: the EVM has a decade of static analyzers, fuzzers, symbolic executors and LLM auditors, while the non-EVM execution models — Solana accounts, Move resources, Cardano's eUTXO, Cairo/Starknet, Algorand/TEAL — have real but siloed per-chain tools and little that crosses chains. This raises a question the field has not answered, or asked rigorously: does vulnerability-detection intelligence transfer across execution models, and if so, which part of it and how far? We propose a falsifiable research program built on an invariant theory of transfer — a vulnerability is the violation of an invariant, and an invariant transfers across execution models exactly to the extent that both models can express the state and control flow it constrains — which predicts the transfer boundary from execution-model semantics rather than from any particular tool. The theory argues against naive unification: the transferable core is small, and the lucrative-looking middle transfers as a class but not as a mechanism. We set out the measurement that can confirm or refute it — independent per-chain corpora, cross-model retrieval and calibration ablations, with pre-registered effect sizes and Wilson 95% confidence intervals — including a publishable negative result. The program states no projected benchmark numbers; every future measurement is an additive, dated artifact, and each chain is a standalone, independently fundable unit. The canonical reproducible claims remain the two RIBCi–CYTED 2026 papers.

    Keywords: smart-contract security · cross-execution-model transfer · invariant theory · vulnerability taxonomy · retrieval-augmented generation · calibration transfer · EVM · Solana · Move · Cardano eUTXO · Cairo/Starknet · Algorand

    1. The question the field skipped¶

    Smart-contract security tooling is sharply asymmetric. The EVM has a decade of static analyzers, fuzzers, symbolic executors and, lately, LLM auditors. The non-EVM world — Solana, Move (Sui/Aptos), Cairo/Starknet, Cardano, Algorand — has real but siloed per-chain tools (the Move Prover [6]; static/fuzzing tools such as VRust for Solana [5]; Caracal for Cairo [7]; Tealer for Algorand [10]; emerging formal-verification work for Cardano) and little that crosses chains. MIESC lives on exactly this frontier: its EVM path is mature (reproducible evidence — SmartBugs 95.8% recall on the LLM-free profile; 11 DeFi incidents 81.8%), its Cairo and Polkadot paths integrate real external analyzers (Caracal; the resolc compiler plus seven polkavm_diff heuristics), and the remaining non-EVM adapters are explicitly pattern-only and Roadmap-stage (docs/MULTICHAIN.md). The asymmetry is the backdrop for this program (Figure 1).

    Faced with that asymmetry, the field has quietly adopted one of two untested assumptions:

    1. Detection is per-chain. Every new chain means a hand-written rule set from scratch — the status quo for the non-EVM analyzers, MIESC's included.
    2. Detection generalizes. A good detector, knowledge base, or LLM "just works" on the next chain.

    Neither has been measured across execution models. If (2) held even partially, the marginal cost of securing a new chain would collapse; if it fails, the "multi-chain security tool" category is, for its non-EVM part, largely unvalidated. This program's thesis is that the truth is in between, and predictable — and that, to our knowledge, nobody has drawn the line with evidence.

    Figure 1. The tooling asymmetry. The EVM has a mature, layered and cross-validating detection stack; the non-EVM world has sparse, siloed per-chain tools with gaps between them. The open question is whether detection intelligence transfers across execution models; this program's thesis is that it transfers partially and predictably — neither "per-chain" nor "generalizes".

    Scope and non-claims. This is not a claim that MIESC has a "unified brain" for all chains, and it contains no projected benchmark numbers. The reasoning below argues against naive unification. Every quantitative claim about MIESC today is traceable to source (benchmarks/results/paper*_claims_matrix.json) or stated as a preliminary, method-limited observation (§3). Every future number will be an additive, dated artifact. Each chain's work is a standalone, independently fundable unit — no result depends on buying the whole program. The canonical reproducible claims remain the two RIBCi–CYTED 2026 papers.

    2. Where this sits — related work and the gap¶

    The adjacent literature is real but stops short of the question, consistently.

    EVM-bound RAG/LLM auditing (crowded). A fast-growing line pairs a vector store of known vulnerabilities with an LLM — e.g. Yu's RAG+GPT-4 auditor over 830 vulnerable contracts (62.7% guided / 60.7% blind, human review still advised) [1] — plus several multi-agent retrieval pipelines. All Solidity/EVM. MIESC's own class-indexed RAG (93 vulnerability documents, SWC/CWE-keyed, no chain field) is one more point in this EVM-shaped cloud. RAG-for-vulnerabilities is neither novel nor cross-chain.

    Cross-language transfer — inside one execution model. The closest transfer claim is a zero-shot detector for Vyper trained on Solidity only, over three vulnerability types (Hu, Zeng & Luo [2]). Solidity and Vyper are different languages on the same EVM; the transfer never crosses an execution-model boundary.

    Non-EVM LLM detection — but on synthetic, self-made data. Boi & Esposito [3] evaluate prompt-engineering vs fine-tuning for LLM detection on Solana (Rust) and Algorand (PyTeal) — and had to build their own synthetic labeled dataset, noting that labeled datasets for non-EVM platforms are lacking. They conclude detection is viable with domain-specific data, and that platform architecture shapes how vulnerabilities appear and can be detected — which both confirms the ground-truth gap this program targets and hints at the execution-model dependence §4 formalizes, though their evidence rests on self-authored synthetic data.

    Taxonomies — one or two models at a time. EVM catalogs (the SWC Registry [8]; hierarchical taxonomies such as OpenSCV [9]) and a MoveEVM Weakness Classification of 37 types for Move-in-EVM (MWC, Topal [4]) cover at most two adjacent models. We found no published taxonomy that spans the account, resource, eUTXO and Cairo models together (as of 2026-11), and execution-model security is otherwise discussed only conceptually (UTXO-vs-account trade-offs), without detection tooling or transfer measurement.

    Table 1. Where the adjacent literature stops. No prior line frames cross-execution-model transfer as a measurable question answered with independent data.

    Prior work Covers Crosses an execution model? Independent corpus?

    Solidity-only zero-shot [2] Solidity → Vyper No (both EVM) —

    To our knowledge, the gap is that cross-execution-model transfer has not been framed as a measurable question, answered with independent data, or predicted from execution-model semantics.

    Positioning drawn from targeted literature searches on 2026-11-13 — cross-chain vulnerability taxonomies, non-EVM transfer learning, RAG-based auditing, and Solana / Move / Algorand benchmarks — not an exhaustive survey.

    3. A preliminary look — what cross-chain detection is today¶

    Before theorizing transfer, we looked at where it is supposed to already exist: inside MIESC's own non-EVM adapters. This section is motivating, not conclusive. A clean per-label verdict requires tracing every emission path — enum references and the pattern-string path each adapter also uses — and a reliable, reproducible version of that inventory is Track T1, not a result claimed here. A quick grep conflates vulnerability labels with opcode and regex constants and must not be trusted; below we report only what survives a careful trace or is trivially checkable.

    Two signs are cleanly reproducible and already telling:

    • The shared knowledge base is EVM-shaped to the core. MIESC's RAG store holds 93 vulnerability documents, no chain field, and essentially zero non-EVM content — so whatever "shared" vocabulary the adapters declare, the shared evidence behind it is Solidity-only.
    • Some cross-chain "shared" cells have no detector behind them at all. Algorand declares no DoS/unbounded-resource label, so a notional cross-chain "shared DoS" has nothing to rest on there; its real update-exposure detector sits under a different label entirely.

    Everything beyond those two needs care: a naive grep conflates vulnerability labels with opcode and regex constants, and several adapters emit through a pattern-string path the enum trace misses — so a trustworthy active-vs-declared inventory per chain (how much of each adapter's declared vocabulary a detector actually produces) is Track T1, not a number claimed here. What inspection suggests, and T1 must confirm, is that declared labels run well ahead of active detectors and that the active ones concentrate in Cairo — which is itself regex-based, with the strong external analyzer (Caracal [7]) outside this label audit entirely.

    What this does and does not show. A declared-but-inactive label shows a detector was not built — not that the class cannot transfer. And a serious rival explanation confounds the pattern: Cairo and Solana received focused effort (Cairo's M1/M2 corpus work and Caracal correlation; a Solana hardening pass), while the other adapters got scaffolding. "Detectors cluster where the invariant allows transfer" and "detectors cluster where effort went" predict the same picture here. Disentangling them is precisely what an independent, effort-controlled measurement (T1–T4) is for.

    So the preliminary picture — nominal labels far ahead of active detectors, concentrated where work was done — is enough to motivate the program and to warn against assuming cross-chain commonality. It is not evidence for the theory of §4; T1 tests that.

    4. An invariant theory of transfer (predictions to be tested)¶

    The engine of this program is one claim: a vulnerability is the violation of an invariant, and an invariant transfers across execution models exactly to the extent that both models can express the state and control flow the invariant constrains. The table is the theory's prediction, to be confirmed or refuted by T1–T4 — both the transfer column and the "models that realize it" column are claims to verify, not established facts. It separates three things usually conflated, which transfer independently: the class (the concept), the mechanism (the concrete detection primitive), and the calibration (false-positive and severity reasoning). Figure 2 makes that decomposition concrete.

    Figure 2. The three parts of a detector are predicted to transfer independently. The class (the concept) transfers broadly; the mechanism (the concrete detection primitive) is substrate-specific and must be rebuilt; the calibration (false-positive and severity reasoning) is predicted not to survive a change of primitive (tested by T3). Worked example: arithmetic overflow — the class is shared, but uint256 reasoning does not carry to a 252-bit Cairo field.

    Table 2. An invariant theory of cross-execution-model transfer. Each row is a prediction from execution-model semantics, to be confirmed or refuted by T1–T4.

    Vulnerability family Invariant it violates Models predicted to realize it Predicted transfer
    Arithmetic / overflow integer/field-width soundness all Class: strong. Mechanism: substrate-specific (solc-0.8 checks ≠ Cairo felt 252-bit field)
    Reentrancy effects-before-interaction under shared mutable state + synchronous re-entry EVM, Solana (CPI), Cairo; absent/rewritten under Move, eUTXO, Algorand Partial — real only where the control-flow invariant exists
    Access control only authorized principals mutate guarded state all (authority is universal) Class transfers; mechanism does not — every model has a different auth primitive
    Oracle / price manipulation external value must be trust-minimized all that read external value Design-level — class transfers; detection needs per-model data sources
    Resource / linearity (hot-potato, capability leak) linear resources are neither duplicated nor dropped linear-type systems (Move) None — no analog off-Move
    eUTXO integrity (double-satisfaction, datum hijacking) each output/validator satisfied exactly once eUTXO (Cardano) None — no analog off-eUTXO

    The "models predicted to realize it" column states the theory's semantic claims — e.g. that eUTXO has no shared mutable state to re-enter — not citations to each runtime's specification; sourcing every cell to primary runtime documentation is itself part of T1.

    Note the correction this forces on our own prior framing: Cairo's felt_overflow is not a chain-specific class — MIESC's taxonomy already collapses it into the shared ARITHMETIC class (finding_taxonomy.py). It is the textbook case of class transfers, mechanism does not: a 252-bit prime field is arithmetic, but nothing about uint256 reasoning carries to it.

    Two consequences follow, each testable:

    • The transferable core is small; the lucrative-looking middle is a trap. Arithmetic transfers as a class; linear-resource and eUTXO families never will; the large access-control/oracle middle transfers as a class but demands per-model mechanism and data — precisely where a naive unified tool loses precision.
    • Transfer is a property of the invariant, not the tool. The engineering question becomes "identify the invariant, then ask whether the target model can host it" — not "port the detector."

    If the theory holds, it explains why honest multi-chain tools degrade off-EVM and hands practitioners a map of where sharing pays. If it fails — if invariant-shared classes do not transfer, or invariant-absent ones do — that is itself a finding about the field's assumptions.

    Figure 3. The invariant matrix. For each vulnerability family and the invariant it violates, the matrix marks which execution models can realize that invariant (filled = realizable; hollow = absent or structurally different) and the predicted transfer — a prediction from execution-model semantics, to be measured by T1–T4, not an established result.

    5. The research program¶

    Each track states a measurable exit criterion and an explicit negative-result path, and decomposes per chain so any one ecosystem can fund and verify its slice. No track asserts a number in advance. The dependencies are deliberately few (Figure 4): only T4 gates another track; T1 and T5 stand alone.

    Figure 4. The program as five tracks. T4 — independent per-chain corpora — is the gate: no quantitative claim is made for a chain until its corpus clears T4, so T4 gates T2 and T3. T1 (commonality inventory and taxonomy) and T5 (methodology generalization) are independent and stand alone. Each track carries an exit criterion and an explicit negative-result path.

    T1 — The commonality inventory + execution-model-aware taxonomy¶

    Do the §3 audit properly: a full, reproducible emission inventory per adapter (enum and pattern paths, mapped to canonical classes), controlling for effort. Then re-express the 16-member CanonicalCategory as an invariant-indexed taxonomy separating class-transfer from mechanism-transfer and flagging label-only matches. - Exit: a published, reproducible active-vs-declared inventory per chain, plus a taxonomy whose real-vs-nominal split matches §4's predictions or refutes them. - Negative path: if the partition does not predict the inventory once effort is controlled, the theory is wrong and is revised against the data.

    T2 — Cross-execution-model retrieval (does the KB transfer?)¶

    Add chain/invariant provenance to the RAG documents; measure whether cross-model retrieval helps for invariant-shared classes only. - Exit: retrieval relevance on a labeled non-EVM query set vs a per-chain baseline; per MIESC's own source policy, RAG context may not create a benchmark claim by itself, so any detection claim is gated on T4. - Negative path: no measured lift → the KB stays per-chain for that class.

    T3 — Calibration transfer (does FP reasoning survive a change of primitive?)¶

    MIESC's FP learner is miesc/ml/fp_ml_classifier.py (scikit-learn; the main fp_filter.py is pattern + RAG, Solidity-keyed). A trained model is not shipped — a stock install runs the rule-based fallback — so T3 both ships a trained artifact and tests whether EVM-learned calibration survives off-EVM. §4 predicts it mostly will not. - Exit: precision/recall on an independent non-EVM labeled set with Wilson 95% CIs vs the per-chain baseline, one comparison and one metric pre-registered; recall preserved (the off preset never filters). - Negative path: underperforms → per-chain calibration, documented as predicted.

    T4 — Independent per-chain corpora (the gate, and the fundable unit)¶

    The program is only as credible as its ground truth — and, as Boi & Esposito [3] show, the field currently resorts to synthetic self-made data. Generalize the methodology scoped for Cairo (the M2 benchmark-corpus plan): independence (no author-labeled N=1 fixtures, no analyzer's own test suite), per-entry provenance + AGPL-compatible license, patched negatives for precision, Wilson 95% CIs, and a pre-registered effect size and sample size so a null is informative, not merely underpowered. - Exit: a credible, independently-sourced corpus per chain before any recall/precision claim for that chain. Each corpus is the artifact its ecosystem can fund and audit. - Negative path: no credible corpus → the chain stays Experimental and makes no quantitative claim. This track gates T2 and T3.

    T5 — Methodology generalization¶

    Apply the defense-in-depth layering and recall-first triage uniformly; where a layer has no analog on a chain (e.g. EVM symbolic execution on a non-EVM target), document the gap rather than paper over it. - Exit: a per-chain layer-coverage matrix, published as a dated artifact. - Negative path: a layer that cannot be realized is marked N/A with the reason.

    6. Contributions and threats to validity¶

    The claim to novelty is specific and bounded. It is not "one brain for all chains" (we reject that). It is three things the field does not have:

    1. A reproducible commonality inventory (the real T1) — the first measured account of how much cross-chain "shared-class" detection is actually implemented versus merely declared, effort controlled.
    2. An invariant theory that predicts the transfer boundary in advance (§4) — turning cross-chain security from porting rules into reasoning about which model can host which invariant.
    3. Independent, per-chain corpora (T4) that make every claim auditable by the ecosystem that cares most — the alternative to synthetic self-made ground truth.

    Figure 5. The falsification test as a controlled ablation. Holding the corpus (which has cleared T4), the detector and the metric fixed, only the EVM-derived knowledge base and calibration are toggled. The difference is measured with Wilson 95% CIs on the contested-middle classes, where H1 predicts a gain, and on invariant-absent classes, which act as a control where none is expected. Arithmetic is never pitted against eUTXO — that gap is true by construction. On the Cairo pilot the invariant-absent control is vacuous (those classes are absent on Cairo), so exercising it needs a Move or Cardano corpus.

    Falsification is built in, tied to a concrete test (Figure 5). The object under test is an ablation: on an independent corpus for a target non-EVM chain, the same detector is run with and without the EVM-derived knowledge base and calibration, against a per-chain-only baseline. The null (H0) is that the EVM-derived intelligence yields no recall or precision gain on the contested-middle classes — reentrancy, access control, oracle — the ones §4 predicts transfer as a class but not as a mechanism. Our bet (the alternative, H1) is a measurable gain for those classes and not for invariant-absent ones. We deliberately do not pit arithmetic against eUTXO integrity: §4 makes that gap true by construction, so it could not fail. A well-powered T4 study that fails to reject H0 undercuts the "detection generalizes" assumption; the Q2–Q4 pilots can only rule a large effect in or out, not settle a tight null.

    Threats to validity. Building independent corpora for several execution models is hard; the program is decomposed per chain and expects to land one model at a time. The first corpus increment (Cairo M2, ~10–15 contracts) is a pilot, sized for a large effect, not a tight null. The §3 effort confound is real and is addressed by the effort-controlled inventory of T1. And neutrality is a requirement, not a slogan: a tool read as "EVM wrapping other chains" will not — and should not — earn non-EVM trust. Depth per model, in the open, is the price of admission.

    7. Milestones — a Cairo pilot and an open agenda¶

    The only timeline committed here is an author-run pilot on one model — Cairo/Starknet, whose corpus is already scoped. Because Cairo is account-based, that pilot can speak to the arithmetic and "class-transfers / mechanism-doesn't" rows, but not to the eUTXO or linear-resource rows. Every other chain is an open invitation, not a personal deliverable: a self-contained T4 corpus that its own ecosystem or outside contributors can fund and run on their own schedule. This is a research agenda to join, not a solo sprint to finish — which is also what keeps it neutral.

    Table 3. The Cairo pilot timeline. Each milestone lands as a dated artifact; none overwrites paper evidence. Chains beyond Cairo extend the same template as independent, contributable slices.

    Quarter Milestone Additive dated artifact
    Q1 T1 reproducible emission inventory (effort-controlled) + invariant taxonomy; §4 predictions registered for the chains it does not yet cover inventory report + invariant taxonomy + prediction ledger
    Q2 First independent corpus clears T4 for Cairo (pilot power stated) labeled corpus + provenance/license ledger
    Q3 T2 cross-model retrieval measured against that corpus retrieval-relevance report (per-chain baseline vs shared)
    Q4 T3 calibration-transfer pilot on Cairo, with Wilson CIs transfer study — first measured cross-model point (positive or null)

    The Q1–Q4 milestones above are the Cairo pilot; each lands as a dated file under docs/research/ or benchmarks/results/, and none overwrites paper evidence. Chains beyond Cairo extend the same template as independent, contributable slices. The §3 audit was exploratory; the registered predictions are what T1 holds to account.

    8. Relation to the existing papers and roadmap¶

    This program is additive future work on the two RIBCi–CYTED 2026 papers — Paper 1 (multi-layer detection) and Paper 2 (verifiable remediation), both EVM/Solidity. It does not restate or revise their numbers. The platform-alignment plan (docs/roadmap/PAPER_LEARNINGS_PLATFORM_ALIGNMENT.md) is EVM-scoped; the cross-chain direction lives in docs/MULTICHAIN.md and docs/ROADMAP.md, which this document extends.
